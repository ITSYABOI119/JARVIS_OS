**Lead — ⚠️ Five consecutive briefings (Sep 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9) reported "nothing has been published out of the seL4 Summit." That is FALSE and has been the whole time: the full program *and* every abstract are on `sel4.systems`, and the LF schedule mirror carries them too. The checks were aimed at the news page, the announce list and YouTube — none of which is where a program lives. And the material includes the single most on-path seL4 item in months: `Single Kernel Multicore seL4` (SKMS), Riverside Research, Day 3.** SKMS is a proof-of-concept **on x86** of an asymmetric-multiprocessing microkernel architecture: the seL4 kernel runs on **one** core; userspace processes are loaded on the *other* cores into address spaces **with no mappings to the kernel at all**; a **remote system call** forwards their requests to the kernel core, and **remote scheduling** lets the single kernel scheduler place processes on those cores. The POC implements exactly three things — memory isolation, remote syscalls, remote scheduling — and the talk presents "a preliminary design idea for leveraging this architecture in *proving* a version of seL4 that can schedule processes across many cores," explicitly asking for community feedback. **Why this is your item and not a curiosity:** JARVIS's deployed x86-64 build is `KernelFastpath=ON` + SMP `NUM_NODES=6`, which `PHASE_4_GOAL1_INFERENCE_PERF.md` §3 records as *outside* the verified configuration by deliberate choice — and the verified estate got further away from you last month when AArch64 completed the confidentiality proof. SKMS is the first concrete proposal I have seen for a **verifiable** multicore seL4, and it is being prototyped on the architecture you actually run. It is also, structurally, the K/M4 carry-forward #3 question (a dedicated core for PA) turned inside out: instead of pinning a userspace process to its own core, pin the *kernel* to one and let userspace own the rest. Prototype, not a port; the honest read is "watch this," and the video is the thing to catch when recordings post. ## seL4 / Microkernels

*(All four items below are from the same never-read Summit program. Videos are **still** not up — the program page promises them on the [seL4 YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@seL4) "after the event," and the 2026 playlist does not exist yet, so abstracts are the record for now.)*

- **Formal verification is being written into EU law's technical scaffolding, and seL4's Foundation CEO did it herself.** June Andronick reports that she submitted **three contributions to the EU Cyber Resilience Act harmonised standard for Operating Systems (EN-304-626)** — requiring formal verification to establish **(1) functional correctness, (2) integrity, (3) confidentiality** — and **all three have been accepted** (standard still in finalisation, no changes requested). Her stated motive is the sharp part: before this, formal verification appeared **nowhere** in the OS standard, which meant a formally verified OS *"would still require additional effort to produce lower assurance evidence, such as being checked for memory errors using a source code analysis tool"* — i.e. verification was being **penalised**, not credited. CRA is fully applicable **December 2027**. Nothing to do; but note the direction of travel makes *which configuration you run* a sharper question than it was, since the three mitigations map onto exactly the three proofs — and your x86-64 build sits outside all of them. [abstract](https://sel4.systems/Summit/2026/abstracts2026.html#a-formal-verification)
- **Update (the Sep-2 lead's stated gap): the timing-channel hole in the confidentiality proof has a live workstream, and it just got unstuck.** Rob Sison (UNSW) reports "a crucial rethink of our approach to verifying a key aspect of time protection for seL4 down to its C implementation, which has **eliminated the main cause of the project's previously large number of breakages to seL4's existing correctness proofs**" — the thing that had made time-protection verification expensive. Separately they implemented time-protected cross-domain shared-memory and notification mechanisms on **RISC-V Cheshire** such that one-way communication is *truly* one-way, "without permitting any timing channels in either (but most importantly, the backwards) direction." Directly relevant framing for your PA↔PB shared pages and notification objects: the property is not free, is not implied by capability separation, and is currently unverified on any platform. [abstract](https://sel4.systems/Summit/2026/abstracts2026.html#a-time-protection)
- **"Efficient User-level Physical Memory Management on seL4" is the talk that reads like it was written about your allocator.** Guangtao Zhu (UNSW) uses physical memory management as a case study to show "the wrong design can make dynamic memory allocation expensive by **placing capability-related operations and metadata bookkeeping directly on the allocation critical path**," and proposes the inverse: keep the fast path small, move capability-heavy work off it — ahead of time or asynchronously — implemented in the seL4 library OS and measured against baselines. JARVIS is at the far end of that spectrum: `sel4utils_bootstrap_..._leaky` is forward-only with **zero teardown primitives**, which is precisely why K/M2 respawn is *reuse-in-place by mandate* rather than by preference. This does not hand you a fix, but it is the first ecosystem work I have seen that treats the allocator's cost structure as the design problem rather than an accepted tax. [abstract](https://sel4.systems/Summit/2026/abstracts2026.html#a-efficient-user-level)
- Also worth a line, both Day 3: **"Booting Windows on an x86-64 seL4-based VMM"** (Bill Nguyen, UNSW) — LionsOS now has a functional **x86-64** VMM, with the talk contrasting Linux's predictable boot against a Windows guest's "strict firmware assumptions and opaqueness"; and **"A policy-free boot process for seL4"** (Daniel Schwyn, Neutrality). x86-64 seL4 getting real userspace investment is the standing gap in your ADR, so both are worth the recordings. [program](https://sel4.systems/Summit/2026/program.html)

## Systems / OS

- **Best pure-workflow item of the day: 23 patches posted Sep 8 by ARM's Lorenzo Stoakes cut Linux kernel build time ~36% (full, all modules), up to ~70% (incremental) and ~90% (noop) — by attacking single-threaded bottlenecks in Kbuild, kallsyms, modpost, objtool, mksysmap and the Rust build system.** The disclosure is the interesting half: an LLM found the bottlenecks and wrote the fixes, "a lot of code, much of it hideous," which he then "extensively audited and rewrote," with every commit carrying an `Assisted-by` tag. That is the first *constructive* entry in the AI-churn thread these briefings have tracked since early August (Greg KH's "rough cycle", the staging ban, the canaries) — same tooling, opposite outcome, and the difference is the audit. Practical read for you: your own loop is dominated by `build_jarvis_x86.sh` + ninja on the box, not the Linux tree, so there is nothing to pull — but "the single-threaded parts of the build were the cost" is a hypothesis worth ten minutes against your own build the next time a box gate feels slow. [Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/AI-To-Faster-Linux-Kernel-Comp) · [patch series](https://lore.kernel.org/rust-for-linux/20260908-build-speedup-v1-0-5dc1ac01672d@kernel.org/)
- **AMD posted a GCC patch (Sep 9) for "Extended Load Optimization" — and notably it is a *general* x86_64 optimization, not `znver`-gated.** It spots a load-in-narrower-type followed by arithmetic-in-wider-type inside the SLP tree and narrows the load vector type where the target has fused load-and-extend, instead of GCC's current `vextract` sequences. Under review for GCC 17, **no performance numbers published**. Flagged rather than sold: the pattern it targets — narrow load, widen, arithmetic — is *exactly* the shape of a dequant-then-dot inner loop, so it is the first compiler change in a while that could plausibly touch hand-written `qdot` codegen rather than only ISA levels you do not have. Would need measuring, and GCC 17.1 is ~Mar–Apr 2027 anyway. [Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/AMD-GCC-Extended-Load-Opt) · [patch](https://gcc.gnu.org/pipermail/gcc-patches/2026-September/730496.html)

## Local LLM Inference

- **Tip is `b10864` (Sep 8): a server checkpoint-eviction fix (#28302) whose failure mode is a recurrent-state one.** `create_checkpoint()`'s spacing rule kept the oldest checkpoint and erased every later one within `checkpoint_min_step` of it — so for prompts *shorter* than `checkpoint_min_step` it dropped the checkpoint at `n_tokens - 4` that the next request resumes from, and **hybrid/recurrent models silently re-prefilled from the previous checkpoint**. Now the spacing rule applies only once the list is full, and a same-`n_tokens` checkpoint is replaced rather than duplicated. Server-path only, so nothing to lift — but it is the *fourth* recurrent-state-lifecycle fix in ten days (delta-net snapshot planes, Qwen4 MTP rollback, Kimi-K3 rollback, now checkpoint retention), and every one has been "the state you resume from was not the state you thought."
